# “They Say We’re Fearless”: *Dead or Alive* Developer Interview

**Source:** *Gamest*, “Dead or Alive Special Interview,” supplied scan, pp. 88–92.  
**Translation:** English translation from the Japanese page images, with the supplied PaddleOCR text used as a reading aid. The original has vertical columns; the questions and answers below follow their conversational order. Section headings follow the magazine where legible. Bracketed notes clarify references or uncertain readings.

> **Magazine introduction:** The men taking *Virtua Fighter 3* head on. Where do their ambitions lie? They say the key to setting themselves apart is a perfectly balanced triangle of attacks.

The interview includes **Tomonobu Itagaki** (producer and director), **Kawaguchi** (chief programmer), **Ehara** (chief planner), **Egawa** (chief motion designer), **Suzuki** (chief sound composer), **Komori** (character designer), and **Fujita** (promotion). The magazine interviewer is labeled **Gamest**; **Adios Toshi**, the magazine’s writer covering the game, also joins in.

## Page 1 — The team and the beginning

![Original interview, page 1](DOA1_Gamest_page_01.jpg)

**Gamest:** Let’s begin with introductions. What have you worked on before, and which parts of *Dead or Alive* did you handle?

**Itagaki:** I’m Itagaki, the producer. Fujita is technically a section manager. “Producer” might not explain it very well: I also do something like a director’s job. I meet with the chiefs here and set the direction. And I deal with Adios, your writer. **Everyone:** [Laughs.]

**Gamest:** What other games have you worked on?

**Itagaki:** Of the ones that reached the market, the *Captain Tsubasa* series.

**Gamest:** The console games?

**Itagaki:** Right. Also *Tecmo Super Bowl* and the horse-racing game *Turf Hero*. I was a developer on those, though, rather than their producer. This is the first game I’ve produced that will be released.

**Egawa:** I’m a subsection chief in product development, chiefly responsible for CG. In *DOA*, my main work, in a word, is the motion. I helped design the framework of the system, and early on I was also involved in analyzing the Model 2 hardware. This is the first commercial title I’ve been assigned to, though I’ve been doing a great deal of 3D work already; this is my third year.

**Gamest:** So you had been preparing steadily?

**Egawa:** You could put it that way.

**Kawaguchi:** I’m also a subsection chief in product development, primarily working on programming. About two years ago I worked on *V Goal Soccer*. Before that I worked on console games.

**Itagaki:** Kawaguchi is the most experienced of us.

**Suzuki:** I handled sound, mainly composing the background music and making the sound effects. I’ve worked on *Tecmo Super Bowl* and *Deron Dero Dero*.

**Ehara:** As chief planner, I handled the planning, specifications, tuning, and pretty much everything else in that area. Previously I worked on console games.

**Komori:** I’m in product development, working on CG: modeling, character design, and making materials such as illustrations and lettering. This is my first game at the company.

### Getting started

**Gamest:** How long was the actual development period?

**Itagaki:** The original proposal came together in June 1995, and work began in earnest that September. Everyone here joined from September. In June it was just me and two others, three people altogether, thinking through the proposal, though of course we had other work too.

**Gamest:** Was Model 2 the plan from the beginning?

**Itagaki:** The company president brought in a Model 2 board and said, “Make something with this. I’ve brought you something good.”

**Gamest:** Other companies have 3D fighting games too. What distinguishes yours?

**Itagaki:** We were thinking about how to handle joint locks. In the end we formed a triangle of **strikes, holds or joint techniques, and throws**. That’s the game’s biggest selling point. It became an entirely new kind of play.

### Motion capture

**Gamest:** What was difficult about motion capture?

**Itagaki:** It has strengths and weaknesses. At the captured speed, the characters don’t move crisply, and there is a lot of unnecessary motion. Refining that material took an enormous amount of time.

**Gamest:** Were the performers specialists in martial arts?

**Itagaki:** They were people from JAC [Japan Action Club] with martial-arts experience. They knew how to fight and how to make movement look good.

**Gamest:** The movements are spectacular.

**Itagaki:** The shoot was hard, wasn’t it?

**Ehara:** Incredibly hard. Sometimes I couldn’t get across what we wanted from the JAC performers, so I demonstrated it myself and ended up more exhausted than they were.

## Page 2 — Capture, characters, and the Danger Zone

![Original interview, page 2](DOA1_Gamest_page_02.jpg)

**Gamest:** What was a motion-capture session like?

**Egawa:** We used reflective motion capture. We even asked them to perform moves where you’d think, “You might die if you actually do that.” [Laughs.]

**Itagaki:** They went right up to the limit for us. We captured the motions of people being blown away, for example. [Laughs.] We put out a platform and mats and had somebody else push the performer’s legs. Sometimes they would hit their necks, or get a nosebleed. But they’re professionals: there would immediately be a stand-in ready.

**Gamest:** Did particular performers handle particular characters?

**Itagaki:** We used different people for different characters.

**Gamest:** Some moves aren’t humanly possible. Did you make any animations from scratch?

**Itagaki:** Yes, though we refer to the captured data when we make them.

**Egawa:** We can animate a movement ourselves, but the capture is better as a reference for things like a character’s sensuality or the intangible feel of a motion. You can’t simply put the captured data into the game. It’s a back-and-forth process.

**Gamest:** Tell us a little more about how capture works.

**Itagaki:** We film from six directions. A picture is two-dimensional, but images from three directions let you calculate a position in space; the other cameras are insurance. The performers wear position-tracking markers all over their bodies. A marker can be hidden when they roll over, but because there are no wires, they can act freely. A wired system is more precise, but the wires get tangled. Either way, if we showed Adios the raw action just as it was captured, he’d say, “What on earth is this?” and tear it to pieces.

**Gamest:** Then you work on it by hand?

**Itagaki:** By hand, and with a sense for how to adapt it. A game character’s movement is completely different. Everybody working in this field makes adjustments. Using capture unchanged would be a disaster. Jann Lee’s punch, for example, comes out in about 20 frames total. There aren’t many real people who can throw a punch like that.

### Designing the cast

**Gamest:** Were any of the characters modeled on real people?

**Komori:** There were various inspirations, both on the planning side and the design side.

**Ehara:** Bayman is based around joint locks, so we gathered material on Volk Han and studied those techniques.

**Gamest:** What about the other characters, as far as you can say?

**Ehara:** Jann Lee is Bruce Lee. For Tina, we studied moves and other material, chiefly from the women’s professional wrestler Manami Toyota.

**Gamest:** Ryu is the return of an older character.

**Ehara:** Once we had decided to include a ninja, we talked about bringing back a character from our past.

**Gamest:** He has a modern design.

**Ehara:** The old one didn’t look very cool. [Laughs.]

**Gamest:** How did you decide on the characters’ names?

**Itagaki:** We struggled with them.

**Ehara:** They changed several times.

**Itagaki:** Here’s the original proposal; it has names you won’t recognize. There was Kenji, a karate practitioner, who disappeared. The Muay Thai fighter was called Kelly.

**Ehara:** We came up with about five names for him.

**Itagaki:** I called him Gatsby; that was the name everyone on the team recognized. Ryu Hayabusa was originally Karasu, then Hayate, then Kamui, and so on.

**Gamest:** Was Kasumi always called Kasumi?

**Itagaki:** Yes. Bayman, incidentally, had a different name. There was also a wrestler called Bass, a man and Tina’s father. Originally the female wrestler was someone else. When Bass could no longer appear, his daughter took his place. Kasumi was already there at that point.

**Gamest:** It sounds as though quite a few characters were cut.

**Tecmo team:** Lots of them.

**Fujita:** Famously, even of the characters on the sign for the February AOU show, about two had disappeared. [Laughs.]

**Ehara:** Kelly and Fan Fu [name as it appears in the scan]. The Mongolian wrestler was an interesting one. We dropped him because we couldn’t come up with any moves.

**Itagaki:** Apart from a Mongolian chop, that is. [Laughs.]

**Ehara:** There was a staff fighter too.

**Itagaki:** Goemon! The only one with a weapon.

**Fujita:** Sorry, please keep that last part off the record. [Laughs.]

### The Danger Zone

**Gamest:** Was the Danger Zone there from the beginning?

**Kawaguchi:** At first it wasn’t like the current version, where you bounce up repeatedly and can land lots of combos. We thought it could add a way to play with different parts of the arena: getting pushed to the edge would be dangerous. That was the structure we started with. In the version at the AOU show, the edge of the ring was an area that dealt damage. After seeing the response at AOU, we all decided that wasn’t strong enough, which led to the explosions and launching effect we have now.

**Itagaki:** The bouncing idea probably came from Adios, didn’t it?

**Adios:** I was writing the article and couldn’t find anything more to say, as usual. I had run out of material.

**Adios:** I said I would write that they were testing a bounce, so could they put one in provisionally? And somehow it stayed all the way through.

**Itagaki:** We were stuck too. People were saying it was called the Danger Zone but wasn’t dangerous at all.

### Taking on a giant

**Gamest:** Were you conscious of *Virtua Fighter 3*?

**Itagaki:** It was our biggest rival.

**Gamest:** Did you plan for the release periods to overlap?

**Itagaki:** We could see from the start that they probably would.

**Adios:** *Virtua Fighter 3* has an evade move. Did you consider including one?

**Itagaki:** This is our proposal from last June. [He takes out the proposal mentioned earlier.] It does include evasive movement. I’ve forgotten why we removed it, though.

**Gamest:** Did it overlap with the role of holds?

**Itagaki:** We meant something different by evasion then. At the very beginning, the game had **four fighters at once**. The proposal included evasion, and there’s a diagram at the top.

**Adios:** Showing a circular path.

**Itagaki:** *VF3* has a straight sideways dash. Ours was more like...

**Gamest:** Circling around someone?

**Itagaki:** Exactly. Imagine three of the four fighters tangled together. One dodges an attack, and it hits another fighter instead. We thought that could be fun.

**Gamest:** Sounds complicated.

**Itagaki:** You’ve hit the nail on the head. [Laughs.] We abandoned it. We’d heard *Virtua Fighter 3* was being developed, and wanted an idea startling enough to stand up to it: four fighters seemed amazing. But we dropped the idea after about a month. Mainly, it just wasn’t very fun. That’s when we settled on the present direction.

## Page 3 — Evasion, character physics, and naming the game

![Original interview, page 3](DOA1_Gamest_page_03.jpg)

**Itagaki:** I remember now why we removed evasive movement. Against a very good Lion or Shun player in *Virtua Fighter 2*, you can reach the point where the opponent can’t even touch you. That frightened us.

### The characters’ movement

**Gamest:** What about the movement of the female characters’ breasts? It looks as if you put a lot of work into it.

**Itagaki:** We certainly did.

**Gamest:** Is it different for each character?

**Itagaki:** All their data differs.

**Adios:** Was it motion-captured?

**Everyone:** [Uneasy laughter.]

**Itagaki:** You could say we motion-captured it in a sense. We asked the performer to move in ways that would produce an interesting result. We captured the women’s movements.

**Adios:** Finding a woman who would produce enough movement must have been hard.

**Itagaki:** We weren’t judging the movement of her breasts. We were looking at how her upper body moved and saying yes or no. But the motion happens in an instant, so when someone asked, “Was that one okay?” it was hard to say.

**Ehara:** We did **not** motion-capture the breasts. [Laughs.]

**Itagaki:** I was so caught up in explaining it that I almost believed we had. [Laughs.] Their movement is calculated. What I meant is that, during capture, we paid attention to the motion of the upper body.

**Adios:** So the breasts themselves weren’t captured?

**Itagaki:** Sorry, no. [Laughs.]

### Costume changes

**Adios:** I think I complained about the costumes more than the physics.

**Gamest:** Were the costumes changing all the time?

**Ehara:** Constantly. [Laughs.]

**Fujita:** The characters in the promotional material and the characters moving in the game were always dressed differently.

**Adios:** The changes seem unusually dramatic compared with other companies’ games.

**Gamest:** Looking back through old issues of *Gamest*, you see something different every time.

**Fujita:** At one point the women doing cosplay were confused enough to contact us and ask which costume was the real one.

**Itagaki:** Tina is popular, isn’t she?

**Adios:** Yes: she’s a women’s wrestler, with that physique. People also like the contrast of red and black in her outfit.

**Itagaki:** We deliberately included designs people would disagree about. We want different people to play different characters. We made a more modest outfit for Kasumi because somebody might want to play her but feel embarrassed by the more revealing one. Hayabusa was difficult, though.

**Komori:** We changed him the most.

**Itagaki:** Every generation has its own idea of what a ninja should look like. People in their fifties and sixties know ninjas, as do people in their twenties and thirties and younger players. Everyone has an opinion. We can accommodate that with up to three player-color variations, but we can’t keep adding more.

**Ehara:** I sometimes think we should have kept that blue Hayabusa design from a little while ago.

**Itagaki:** The one with the armor?

**Fujita:** People ask about that. Someone who had made a cosplay outfit called to ask what to do with it.

**Itagaki:** People also ask why the blue Leifang disappeared.

**Fujita:** We still occasionally get calls about blue Leifang and pink Kasumi.

### “DOA? Dead?”

**Gamest:** How did you settle on the title *DOA*?

**Itagaki:** A name matters enormously, so we argued over it.

**Ehara:** We thought about it for about a month, and came up with roughly a hundred possibilities without deciding. For a time we’d even settled on *Buraio* (武雷王), but then thought better of it. [The exact intended reading of the unused title is not printed.]

**Fujita:** When *DOA* came up, it arrived very naturally. The name came after the game. Some opposed it when we first chose it. It’s familiar now, but people said it was hard to shorten, among other things.

### Even in death...

**Gamest:** An abrupt, very specific question: why does Jann Lee turn his eyes away when an attack connects?

**Itagaki:** Perhaps because it has more impact that way.

**Ehara:** Bruce Lee does that. The motion-capture performer was a huge Bruce Lee fan and would turn away during all his actions. That came through in the game.

**Itagaki:** He was a serious fanatic, a veteran who, in a good way, would never do the same movement twice. [Laughs.]

**Ehara:** He’d improvise incredible things without being asked.

**Itagaki:** If you said, “Next, a middle kick,” an ordinary performer would look at the storyboard. He’d turn away and start thinking.

**Ehara:** Even after seeing the storyboard he’d say, “That’s not how Bruce Lee would do it.” [Laughs.]

## Page 4 — Balance, holds, and the voice cast

![Original interview, page 4](DOA1_Gamest_page_04.jpg)

### A new balance

**Adios:** Now, the very worst thing about this game is...

**Everyone:** [Surprised laughter.]

**Adios:** I understand the idea, but the range of the rising kick is incredibly short. I don’t think anyone will use it. If you try to catch someone while they get up, the hold has to work at close range, so I assume that’s why you shortened it. What was the real reason?

**Itagaki:** When we considered shortening it, we first thought of *Tekken*: getting knocked down there is a major problem. The underlying principle is that the player who scores the knockdown deserves the advantage. We had to decide who deserved it here. We reconsidered the kick’s reach, speed, and ability to knock the opponent down, and ultimately took away its knockdown. We extended it a little for characters whose legs are physically short. The rest was something we agonized over until the end, but this is what we settled on.

**Ehara:** If players don’t use rising kicks, I’m happy: then they can fight standing up. For a while *Virtua Fighter 2* felt like a game where players took turns falling down and getting up after rising kicks. We wanted them to fight on their feet, without creating so many knockdown situations.

**Adios:** So you see rising kicks as something that gets in the way of stand-up fighting?

**Ehara:** That’s why they don’t knock you down. A rising kick makes you reel, and combos can start from there, but you stay standing.

**Adios:** These days there are quite a few games where a rising kick makes you stagger, but you were ahead of the curve.

**Ehara:** I was shocked when I played *Last Bronx* and a successful rising kick didn’t knock the other person over.

**Gamest:** I want to praise the way moves with big openings can actually be used. The idea that the other player doesn’t get an attacking turn unless they catch you is impressive. In earlier games, high-damage moves with a lot of recovery were often interrupted before they came out. You wondered what purpose they served. In *DOA*, you can often keep attacking without being punished. Did the hold system start from that line of thinking?

**Ehara:** Yes. We wanted holds to take the place of guarding, in a sense. When you fail to catch the opponent with a hold, counterattacking is difficult. That’s deliberate.

**Gamest:** You can also delay a slower attack to vary its timing. It feels carefully considered.

**Ehara:** Exactly.

**Gamest:** And there’s little recovery on many attacks. Was this triangle already in mind?

**Ehara:** We had the idea for the system, but we had a hard time tuning it. It was only very recently that I felt the balance was right.

**Gamest:** There was a time when the hold’s active window was so long that it would catch almost anything.

**Ehara:** And the opposite extreme, where a missed catch left you open to anything. We tried all sorts of things.

**Gamest:** Did you intend the aggressor to have the advantage? It seems to reward driving an opponent back more than being skilled at sitting on defense.

**Itagaki:** I’d rather a beginner who attacks beat an intermediate player who just waits.

**Ehara:** We set it up to be balanced when skilled players face one another, so at first people will probably say a particular character is absurdly strong.

**Gamest:** Kasumi seems strong initially.

**Adios:** It’s good that there isn’t an excessively strong character.

**Gamest:** I suspect that, as people learn the game, the two characters with follow-up moves off their catches will become very powerful. It’s impressive that this was thought through from the start.

**Ehara:** Gen Fu spent a long time as the weakest character and resisted all our attempts to make him strong. We adjusted the reliable moves after his catches, so I think he can now compete much better.

**Itagaki:** One thing we decided was that when someone was said to be too strong, we wouldn’t weaken that character. We’d strengthen the others. Otherwise the game becomes frustrating. Adios said this too, when Jann Lee and Kasumi were being singled out.

**Ehara:** Back then the joint-lock characters hadn’t been tuned. Even if they caught a limb, they couldn’t do anything. Leifang, for example, got nothing from a deflection, so people stopped using it. Once we adjusted those things, the balance changed markedly. Leifang and Gen Fu, the characters built around catches, were among those who became stronger late in development.

### Voice actors

**Gamest:** How did you choose the voice actors?

**Ehara:** We drew up several candidates who suited our mental picture. Then there were questions about whether we could hire them, and whether a choice I liked would convince everyone at the planning meeting. We discarded those that wouldn’t and selected the best.

**Itagaki:** Adios looks like he wants to say something.

**Fujita:** He came to the recording session.

**Itagaki:** He was awful that day. He arrived late, read an idol directory, ate a boxed lunch, complained, and left.

**Adios:** You make me sound like a terrible person.

**Itagaki:** Being harsh is good: he tells us when something doesn’t work.

**Fujita:** That’s fine with us.

**Itagaki:** We appreciate it.

**Fujita:** Sometimes people from magazines don’t like what they’ve seen but go home praising it anyway. **Everyone:** [Laughs.]

**Gamest:** “Uh, yeah...” [Awkward laugh.] And then, “It was great.”

**Adios:** When I saw the game for the first time, they asked for my honest impression, so I said, “It’s no good.” [Laughs.]

**Fujita:** But without that, I don’t think we’d have kept going.

**Itagaki:** Something bad stays bad no matter how far you take it.

## Page 5 — Secrets, controls, and closing thoughts

![Original interview, page 5](DOA1_Gamest_page_05.jpg)

### Hidden features

**Adios:** Are there any hidden elements?

**Itagaki:** Burst Mode is explained in the demonstration.

**Adios:** Any particularly big moves?

**Itagaki:** Big moves... The last boss has moves of his own, for example. About that much.

**Adios:** Will the last boss become playable?

**Itagaki:** I think it will turn out as you expect.

**Gamest:** With a secret command?

**Itagaki:** Our thinking is that it would be a shame for someone who can’t enter the command.

**Fujita:** Let’s leave it there.

### Back to block, or a button?

**Adios:** How did you decide against using a button to guard?

**Ehara:** We decided from the outset to have a hold button.

**Adios:** *Virtua Fighter 3*, for example, has four buttons.

**Ehara:** That was an option, but four buttons create problems for arcade control panels, and the fourth button is hard to press in the first place.

**Itagaki:** You need a good reason to go from one button to two. Each button carries weight. The same goes for two to three, and even more so for three to four. Still, we agonized over it; everyone we met wanted a guard button.

**Adios:** I’m used to it now, but at first I kept trying to hold right at the opening of a round.

**Itagaki:** What persuaded me was the possibility that someone intending to guard would press the wrong thing and accidentally perform a hold. I’ve heard of this happening to several people; it might draw them naturally into a new way of playing. That’s why we committed to the H button.

**Adios:** Why include jumping attacks?

**Itagaki:** We wanted a jump kick that was useful.

**Ehara:** At first the jump was high, but jumping attacks were unusable because the character leapt so far up. We set about making a practical jump kick.

**Itagaki:** That was one of the hardest things to get right.

**Ehara:** We adjusted the jump height in all sorts of ways.

**Adios:** Eventually you stopped letting characters leap over the opponent.

**Ehara:** There was no reason to.

**Itagaki:** Originally there was a reason, but people didn’t like it.

**Itagaki:** *Street Fighter II* also guards by holding back, right? People call us fearless, but we took inspiration from Capcom for jumping kicks and punches. Our game tends to have more vertical interaction.

### To the readers

**Gamest:** Could you each leave our readers with a comment?

**Ehara:** The catch or hold techniques are our selling point. The better you master them, the stronger they become. Make good use of them.

**Suzuki:** The BGM CD is out. Please give it a listen.

**Kawaguchi:** You may not immediately be able to tell whether the balance of this game is good, but I assure you it is! We finished something good, so please play it thoroughly.

**Egawa:** I’m especially attached to Tina and Bayman. I made a lot of the throws. At first Bayman had moves you’d never believe belonged to a practitioner of combat sambo; we gradually changed them. I put a lot into him, so please use Bayman to flatten someone like Jann Lee.

**Komori:** Tina and Bayman are also the characters I’m most attached to in terms of their designs and moves. Please use them to flatten Kasumi. I’m saying the same thing, aren’t I? [Laughs.]

**Itagaki:** Before I wrap up, let me mention Jann Lee. He has four “Dragon something” moves. If you land all four, that’s what we call a “cycle hit.” [Laughs.] Please try it. More generally, as Kawaguchi said, the balance may be hard to see at first. Initially it may seem that Jann Lee wins just by going “Acha!” and Kasumi wins just by spinning around. That won’t last. Pick the character you find appealing: we’ve made it so that any of them can work if you spend time with them.

**Itagaki:** Kasumi is easy for anyone to pick up, so I recommend her to beginners. We made her for people who had never played a fighting game. It’s natural that she can move around and win a lot at first, but she may have a harder time later on. The old man [Gen Fu], Bayman, and Tina are more technical characters, so players who choose them should practice.

**Gamest:** Thank you all for today.

- **Image files:** The five images are complete scans of the interview pages, in order: `DOA1_Gamest_page_01.jpg` through `DOA1_Gamest_page_05.jpg`. - **Dates:** “June 1995” translates 平成7年6月. “Last June” in the discussion of the proposal refers back to that period.
- **Terminology:** The interview often uses つかみ, literally “catch/grab,” for the counter or hold mechanic. I translate it as “catch” or “hold” according to context. The article’s “triangle” is strikes, holds/joint techniques, and throws. “Rising kick” translates 起きあがり蹴り / 起き蹴り.
- **Uncertain names:** The scan reads the removed character as ファンフー, rendered **Fan Fu** here. The abandoned title is written **武雷王**; its intended pronunciation is not provided. Names in the participant roster and some magazine bylines are difficult to read in the scan, so the dialogue uses surnames.
- **Editorial reconstruction:** The OCR scrambled adjacent vertical columns in several places, particularly the Danger Zone, original four-fighter plan, breast-motion exchange, and control discussion. Those passages were reordered against the page images. Explanatory bracketed notes are the translator’s.
